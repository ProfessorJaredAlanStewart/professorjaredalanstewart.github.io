A Field Guide

Show Up in Fort Worth

Where the meetings are, how to speak, which government actually handles your problem, and how to make sure your vote counts. Everything on this page is free, public, and yours by right.

Every institution on this page belongs to you. You don't need an appointment, an invitation, or a reason — being a resident is the whole credential. Most of my students who attend their first public meeting say some version of the same thing afterward: "I had no idea I was allowed to just... walk in." You are. Here's exactly how, starting with a two-minute map of which government owns which problem.

The law is on your side. The Texas Open Meetings Act requires governmental bodies to meet in public, post their agendas at least 72 hours in advance, and let you watch every deliberation and vote. The Texas Public Information Act gives you the right to request government records. Showing up isn't a privilege they extend — it's an obligation they owe.

Meeting details, phone numbers, and deadlines on this page verified August 2026. Spot something out of date? Email me and I'll fix it — consider it your first act of civic participation.

Who does what: a two-minute map

Start Here

If you learn one thing from this page, learn this: you don't live under one government. A Fort Worth address puts you inside at least seven of them at once, each with its own leaders, its own budget, its own taxes, and its own elections. The City of Fort Worth. Tarrant County. Your school district. The Tarrant County College district. A handful of special districts for water, transit, hospitals, and property appraisal. The State of Texas. The United States.

This layered setup is called federalism: the Constitution splits power between the national government and the states, and the states create every local government beneath them. It sounds like a trivia answer until you need something fixed, because each layer only controls its own lane. Call city hall about your driver's license and they'll politely send you to the state. Ask Washington about a pothole and you'll wait a very long time.

So before you show up anywhere, match your problem to its government. Each badge below jumps to the section that shows you how:

A pothole, a streetlight, your water bill, the police, a zoning fight, the parksCity
Registering to vote, jury duty, the county jail, deeds and marriage licensesCounty
What your house is officially worth, and whether you can fight that numberAppraisal District
Your kid's school, the curriculum, a campus closure, the superintendentSchool District
Classes, tuition, and campuses at Tarrant County CollegeCollege District
The bus, the TEXRail line, the water supply, a bill from JPS hospitalSpecial Districts
Your driver's license, state highways, university tuition, the criminal codeState
Social Security, Medicare, a stuck passport, immigration, the IRS, veterans' benefitsFederal
Pro tip: When the layers collide, that's not a glitch. That's federalism in action. The state's takeover of the Fort Worth ISD school board is the live local example: Austin used its power over a government it created. Watching that fight up close is a civics course all by itself.
Part 1 · Your City

Attend a Fort Worth City Council meeting

City

This is where your property taxes, your streets, your water rates, your parks, and your police budget get decided. Council meets in the new City Hall — the converted Pier 1 tower by the Trinity River — in a chamber built with 250 seats specifically so the public can attend.

Where
Council Chamber, Fort Worth City Hall, 100 Fort Worth Trail, Fort Worth, TX 76102 (just right of the main entrance)
When
Tuesdays, roughly three per month — daytime meetings typically start at 10 a.m. and evening meetings at 6 p.m. Check the city council meetings & agendas page for the exact schedule and posted agenda.
To speak
Fort Worth uses online Speaker/Comment Cards attached to each posted agenda. To get on the list:
  1. Go to fortworthtexas.gov, scroll to the City Council links in the site footer, and open "City Council Meeting and Work Session Agendas."
  2. Search the calendar for your meeting (set it to All Departments if nothing appears) and open the Agenda PDF.
  3. Find the Speaker/Comment Card link under the agenda item you want to address — or under the public comment section for anything else.
  4. Fill out the card. That's it — you're signed up.
As of 2026, speakers get three minutes, and residents fought to win back open comment at the end of regular meetings this year; use it. Full instructions are on the city's how to appear or speak before City Council page, and the City Secretary's office will walk you through it at 817-392-6150.
Can't make it?
Every meeting streams live and is archived on FWTV and the city's YouTube channel. Watching counts as showing up for CAP purposes — but the room hits different.
Your district
Fort Worth has 10 council districts plus a citywide mayor. Find yours on the district map, then contact your council member.
Pro tip: Read the agenda the day before and pick one item to follow. A two-hour meeting becomes a story when you're rooting for (or against) something specific — and locating the Speaker/Comment Card the night before beats hunting for it from the chamber lobby. Bonus: the consent agenda — dozens of items passed in a single vote — is where most of the actual money moves.

Get a pothole fixed (and report everything else)

City

This is the single fastest way to watch your government work for you. Fort Worth's street crews fix roughly a hundred potholes a day, and they aim to repair reported ones within about 48 hours. But here's the catch, straight from the crews themselves: if nobody reports it, they don't know it's there.

Best way
The MyFW app (free on the App Store and Google Play). Snap a photo, drop a map pin, submit. You can track the request's status and get a notification when it's done.
Other ways
Call the city's customer care line at 817-392-1234, text "hello" to 817-835-6939 (MYFW), or submit a request on the city website.
Also works for
Broken traffic signals, missing signs, streetlight outages, graffiti, high grass, illegal dumping, code violations — anything that's the city's job to fix.
Pro tip: Note the exact location and rough size when you report. And screenshot your request — a before/after with the city's "completed" notification is a perfect artifact for a CAP write-up, and an oddly satisfying superpower in general.

Have a say in the city budget

City

Every pothole crew, police shift, and library branch on this page runs on one document: the annual city budget. Fort Worth's fiscal year starts October 1, and the months before that are when residents can actually move money. Show up then and you're not commenting on a decision. You're part of one.

How it works
The city releases a recommended budget in late summer, staff hold community meetings around the city (livestreamed, with Q&A) to explain it, and the council holds a public hearing in September before the vote. Key dates, budget documents, and feedback forms live on the city's budget page.
Follow along
The city's engagement site, ConnectFW, breaks down where the money comes from and where it goes in plain language, and lets you submit questions before the meetings.
Pro tip: The property tax rate gets adopted alongside the budget every fall, and both votes happen whether or not anyone shows up. If you attend exactly one city meeting a year, make it the September budget hearing. It's the one where everything else on this page gets funded, or doesn't.
Part 2 · Your County

Attend Tarrant County Commissioners Court

County

The Commissioners Court — a county judge elected countywide plus four commissioners elected by precinct — runs the county: the hospital district, the jail, county roads, elections, and a multi-billion-dollar budget. Despite the name, it's a governing body, not a courtroom.

Where
Tarrant County Administration Building, 100 E. Weatherford St., Fort Worth, TX 76196 — downtown, about a mile from City Hall
When
Monthly, generally the second Tuesday at 10 a.m., with special meetings as needed. (The court cut back from weekly meetings in late 2025 — a controversial change worth discussing in class.) Agendas post at least three business days ahead on the county's Commissioners Court agendas & public notices page.
To speak
Once the agenda posts, register online via the "Register to Speak" buttons in the posted agenda, or in person at the Administration Building, Suite 404. Deadlines are strict — check the posted agenda for the cutoff. You can also submit written comments on any agenda item.
Can't make it?
Meetings stream live on the county's YouTube channel; recordings post within about a day.
Pro tip: County government is the layer almost nobody watches, which makes it the layer where one informed resident stands out the most. The public comment rules here are stricter than the city's — read the rules of decorum before you go, and watch the clock when you speak.

Register to vote (or make sure you still are)

County

Texas does not have online voter registration for new voters — it's paper, and there's a hard deadline of 30 days before any election you want to vote in. That trips up more of my students than any other rule on this page. Don't be one of them.

Check first
Check your status through the Texas Secretary of State's official VoteTexas.gov portal, which links the "Am I Registered?" lookup. Moved or changed your name? You can update an existing registration online through Texas.gov.
To register
Pick up a postage-paid application at any Tarrant County library, post office, city hall, subcourthouse, or public high school — or print one from Tarrant County Elections — and mail it or drop it off at the Elections Center, 2700 Premier St., Fort Worth, TX 76111. Questions: 817-831-VOTE (8683).
Then vote
Tarrant County uses countywide vote centers — on Election Day you can vote at any center in the county, not just your precinct. Early voting runs for about two weeks before most elections. Locations and sample ballots: Tarrant County Elections.
Work an election
Don't just vote at the polls. Run them. Tarrant County needs roughly 2,500 election workers for a general election, pays for both the work and the training, and takes applications through its poll workers page or at 817-831-6497. High school students 16 and up can serve as student election clerks with an excused absence from school. It's a paid, front-row seat to how democracy actually operates.
Pro tip: The elections nobody shows up for — municipal races, school board, bond packages, constitutional amendments — are decided by a tiny fraction of registered voters. That's not a depressing fact; it's an arbitrage opportunity. Your vote is mathematically never worth more than in a May local election.

Protest your property appraisal

Appraisal District

Here's a fact that surprises almost everyone: no city council or school board decides what your house is worth. That job belongs to the Tarrant Appraisal District (TAD), a separate government most people have never heard of, and you have a legal right to challenge its number every single year. It costs nothing, you don't need a lawyer, and protests regularly end in reductions.

The deadline
Generally May 15, or 30 days after your appraisal notice arrives, whichever is later. Miss it and you wait a year. File online, by mail, or in person through TAD.org.
How it goes
Most protests settle informally with an appraiser. If you don't agree, you get a hearing before the Appraisal Review Board, a panel of ordinary residents. Bring evidence: photos of needed repairs, recent sale prices of similar homes nearby, your own purchase documents.
Exemptions
If you own and live in your home, make sure your homestead exemption is on file with TAD. It's free and lowers your taxable value automatically. Over 65 or disabled? Additional exemptions apply.
Renters
The protest right belongs to the property owner, but rising appraisals flow straight into rent, and the tax rates set each fall by the city, county, school district, and college district are public votes at public meetings. Every one of them takes comment.
Pro tip: Since 2024, some of TAD's board seats are filled by direct election, in May contests almost nobody votes in. You now help choose who oversees the office that values every property in the county. See the voting section above; it's the same registration.
Part 3 · Your Schools & Colleges

Attend a Fort Worth ISD board meeting

School District

School boards decide budgets, school closures, curriculum policy, and superintendent hires — and right now, Fort Worth ISD is one of the most consequential civics case studies in Texas: in March 2026, the Texas Education Agency suspended the elected Board of Trustees' authority and appointed a Board of Managers to govern the district. State power versus local control isn't a textbook hypothetical here. It's Tuesday night.

Where
FWISD District Service Center, 7060 Camp Bowie Blvd., Fort Worth, TX 76116
When
Check the FWISD board meetings page for the calendar and posted agendas.
To speak
After the agenda posts, call 817-814-1920 by 4 p.m. the day of the meeting to sign up, or sign up in person at the meeting (arrive early — in-person signup closes before the meeting starts). Written comments can be emailed to boardmeetings-publiccomment@fwisd.org by noon the day of the meeting.
Can't make it?
Meetings stream live on the Fort Worth ISD YouTube channel and the district's Video on Demand page.
Pro tip: Live outside FWISD? Every school district in Tarrant County — Keller, Crowley, Arlington, Eagle Mountain-Saginaw, and the rest — holds public board meetings under the same Open Meetings Act rules. Your district's website will have the schedule.

Watch the college district too

College District

Tarrant County College is its own government: the Tarrant County College District, with an elected board of trustees, its own property tax, and public meetings under the same Open Meetings Act as everyone else on this page. If you or your kids have ever taken a class at TCC, this board set the tuition.

Where to start
Board meeting schedules and agendas post on tccd.edu (search "board of trustees"). Trustee races are exactly the kind of low-turnout election where a handful of votes decides who runs a taxing district.
Full disclosure: I teach at TCC, so I'm biased. I'd still put this board on the page. Community college districts tax, spend, and educate at a scale most residents never notice.
Part 4 · Your State

Find and contact your state representatives

State

Austin decides more about your daily life than Washington does — your tuition, your driver's license, your criminal code, your property tax system. You have one state representative and one state senator, and their offices answer constituent mail because constituents are the whole job.

Who are they?
Enter your address at Who Represents Me? — the official Texas Capitol lookup. It returns your state rep, state senator, U.S. representative, and State Board of Education member, with contact links.
Track a bill
Texas Legislature Online lets you search any bill, read its text and history, and set up free email alerts when it moves. The Legislature meets in regular session for 140 days in odd-numbered years — the next regular session convenes in January 2027 — but interim committee hearings happen year-round and accept public testimony.
Make contact
A short, specific, polite email or call to the district office about one bill or one issue gets logged and tallied. Form letters get counted; personal stories get remembered.
Pro tip: Want to testify in Austin someday? Watch a committee hearing on Texas Legislature Online first — public testimony is usually two to three minutes, and you'll see that ordinary Texans do it every single day the Legislature is in session.

Part 5 · Your Country

Use your federal government

Federal

Washington feels far away until your passport is stuck, your Social Security payment is wrong, or a VA claim has been "processing" for a year. Then it turns out you have two U.S. senators and a U.S. representative whose offices employ people, called caseworkers, whose entire job is to lean on federal agencies for constituents. This is the most underused free service in American government.

Who they are
Tarrant County is split among several congressional districts, so check yours with the House's official Find Your Representative lookup. Your two senators represent the whole state; contact info is at senate.gov. The state's Who Represents Me? tool covers your U.S. House member too.
Get casework help
Stuck with a federal agency over a passport, Social Security, Medicare, the IRS, immigration, or veterans' benefits? Call your U.S. representative's district office and ask for casework help. You'll sign a privacy release, and a staffer will make inquiries you can't make yourself. It's free, it's routine, and it's a core part of the job.
Track and comment
Congress.gov tracks every federal bill and lets you read exactly what your representative voted for. And when federal agencies write new rules, they're required to take public comments first at Regulations.gov. Anyone can file one, and agencies are required to consider them.
See it in person
Federal courts are open to the public. Fort Worth's Eldon B. Mahon U.S. Courthouse (501 W. 10th St.) hosts trials, appeals, and, best of all, naturalization ceremonies, where a federal judge swears in new American citizens. Schedules and visiting rules are on the Northern District of Texas site. Go once. Everyone cries.
Pro tip: A caseworker can't change the rules, but they can make the system respond. Call early, come organized (names, dates, case and confirmation numbers), and be kind to the staffer. They didn't lose your passport. They're the one getting it back.
Part 6 · Every Level

Request public records

Any Level

Under the Texas Public Information Act, you can request records from any Texas governmental body — emails, contracts, budgets, reports — and they generally must respond promptly, with most responses due within 10 business days. One of my students used this to investigate the billion-dollar Panther Island project. His first request. It worked.

City records
Fort Worth accepts public information requests through the City Secretary's office and its online records portal — start at the City Secretary page.
County records
Tarrant County's public information request process lives on tarrantcountytx.gov — search "public information request."
How to ask
Be specific and narrow: a date range, a department, a document type. "All emails about X between January and March" beats "everything about X." You don't have to explain why you want it — the law doesn't require a reason.
Pro tip: Before filing a request, check whether it's already public — council agendas, budgets, check registers, and crime data are often posted online. The best records request is the one you didn't have to file.

Meet your hidden governments

Special Districts

Texas runs on special districts: single-purpose governments with real taxing or spending power and almost no audience. Several shape daily life in Fort Worth, and every one of them holds public meetings.

Water
The Tarrant Regional Water District manages the region's water supply, the Trinity River levees, and the Panther Island project, with an elected board chosen in some of the lowest-turnout elections in the county. Board meetings are public and posted on its site.
Transit
Trinity Metro runs the buses and the TEXRail line to DFW Airport. Its board is appointed rather than elected, which makes public meetings and rider feedback the main way in.
Health care
The JPS Health Network is the Tarrant County Hospital District: a government that taxes county property to fund the public hospital system. Its board meets publicly (start at jpshealthnet.org), and the Commissioners Court appoints that board, which makes the county the pressure point.
Appraisal
The Tarrant Appraisal District values every property in the county. It's the special district you're most likely to tangle with, so it gets its own section above.
Pro tip: Special district decisions rarely make the news, which is exactly why one prepared resident at a meeting matters more here than anywhere else in local government. When my students want to find a story nobody else is covering, I send them here first.

More ways in

All Levels

Showing up has more doors than a council chamber:

Serve on a board
Fort Worth has dozens of resident boards and commissions — zoning, parks, libraries, community development — and they regularly need volunteers. Browse openings and apply through the city's boards and commissions page. Yes, regular people get appointed. That's the point.
Answer jury duty
It's the one form of civic participation that comes with a summons — and it's the only time most citizens directly exercise judicial power. Don't dodge it; bring a book and pay attention. It's the best free civics seminar in the county.
Get a library card
The Fort Worth Public Library is free, and it's quietly one of the city's best civic institutions: meeting rooms, research databases, tax help, voter registration forms, and librarians who can find anything.
Join your neighborhood
Neighborhood associations are where city hall actually meets your street. The city's Neighborhood Services department keeps a directory — and if your neighborhood doesn't have one, starting one is itself a civic act.
Follow local news
You can't show up to what you don't know about. The Fort Worth Report (nonprofit, free) and KERA News cover city hall, the county, and the school district week in, week out.
Know the numbers
Three numbers cover most of life: 911 for emergencies, 817-392-1234 (or the MyFW app) for city services, and 2-1-1 for the state's free helpline that connects people to food, housing, utility, and health assistance. Handing the right number to a neighbor counts as civic participation too.

Your first meeting: a checklist

Print this. Or don't. Just go.
  • Read the agenda the night before. They're posted at least 72 hours out, by law. Pick one item to follow.
  • Arrive 15 minutes early. Public buildings have security lines, and seats up front go first.
  • You don't have to speak. Sitting and watching is fully showing up. Nobody will call on you.
  • There's no dress code. Come from work, come from class, come as you are.
  • Take notes. Who voted how, what surprised you, what you'd ask. (My students: this is your CAP write-up.)
  • Introduce yourself to one person. A council member, a staffer, a fellow resident. Civic life runs on hello.
