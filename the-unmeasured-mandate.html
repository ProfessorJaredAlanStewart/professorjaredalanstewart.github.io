Dissertation in Practice · Ed.D. Stewart, J. A. · Tarleton State University In re: Tex. Educ. Code § 51.301

Texas Education Code § 51.301 · enacted 1929

“Every college and university receiving state support… shall give a course of instruction in government or political science… This course shall have a credit value of not less than six semester hours… a college or university… may not grant a baccalaureate degree or a lesser degree or academic certificate to any person unless the person has credit for such a course.”
Never Measured

Texas built the classroom in 1929.
No one ever checked whether it works.

For almost a century, every degree earned at a Texas public college has required six credit hours of government. The state calls civic capability an intended outcome of those hours. This dissertation asks the question nobody has answered: do the courses deliver? And it tests a redesign built for the students who need the answer to be yes.

The premise underneath all of it: civic capability is built, not inborn. Nobody arrives knowing how to work a city council or read a ballot — a classroom builds that, or nobody does. So the real question was never whether these students are apathetic. It’s whether the course is doing its job.

Exhibit A · The Mandate

The state already decided this matters.

This project doesn’t begin with an opinion about civics. It begins with a law.

Since 1929, Texas has required six semester hours of government or political science for every degree at a public institution. Community college students meet the requirement through two courses: GOVT 2305 (Federal Government) and GOVT 2306 (Texas Government). Then the Texas Higher Education Coordinating Board tells those courses what they exist to produce. Among the required core objectives is social responsibility: “knowledge of civic responsibility, and the ability to engage effectively in regional, national, and global communities.”

Tex. Educ. Code § 51.301; Texas Higher Education Coordinating Board, 2018

Read those two documents together and the conclusion is unavoidable: the government course is not trivia for a transcript. It is democratic infrastructure, by law. The state guarantees every single graduate one structured encounter with civic learning, and it happens in this course or it happens nowhere.

Which raises the question this whole project turns on: is the infrastructure actually working?

Exhibit B · The Gap

The evidence says: probably not. And not for everyone equally.

I have taught these courses for years, and I can tell you what the traditional version produces: students who can name the three branches on a scantron and still feel like strangers to the government running their lives. The national data says my classroom is not the exception.

Civic participation in America has been sliding for half a century, and the slide is steepest among the young. But the decline is not evenly spread, and that unevenness is the heart of this project.

50% → <33% Youth voting (ages 18–24), 1972 to the late 1990s. The habit of participation is eroding fastest where it should be forming. Westheimer & Kahne, 2004
The middle child Community college students consistently land between high-school graduates and four-year students on voting, volunteering, and news habits. Lopez & Brown, 2006; Kisker et al., 2016
1/10th Civic-engagement staffing per student at commuter campuses versus residential ones, across 1,100+ institutions, even after controlling for money, size, and selectivity. Evans et al., 2019

Tarrant County College is a commuter, open-access, Hispanic-Serving Institution. It is a near-exact portrait of the campus type that research identifies as poorest in civic opportunity. There is no residence hall, no dense club culture, no civic-engagement office with deep staffing to pick up the slack. For TCC students, the required government course isn’t one civic opportunity among many. It is carrying nearly the entire load.

Exhibit C · The Mechanism

Civic engagement is taught, not inherited.

This is the hinge the whole argument turns on: the fact that converts a civic worry into an educational problem, and an educational problem into something a classroom can fix.

The chance to argue a current event, work a community issue, or practice speaking in public does not fall from the sky. Schools distribute those chances, and which students get them is no accident. It tracks race and family income. Researchers call it the civic opportunity gap: students of color and students from lower-income homes receive fewer of the classroom experiences known to build engagement. Follow the gap downstream and it becomes a civic empowerment gap in knowledge, skill, and confidence that runs along the same lines.

Kahne & Middaugh, 2008; Levinson, 2012

Benjamin Barber put the underlying principle plainly decades ago: “citizens are certainly not born, but made.” Nobody arrives civically capable. Somebody teaches you, or nobody does.

Barber, 1984

Schools built this gap —
so schools can close it.

That’s the part that should make anyone in education sit up — and the evidence says the tools work. Classroom civic learning builds lasting commitment to participation more effectively than general academic support does, with meaningful effects among exactly the low-income students and students of color the system has shortchanged.

Kahne & Sporte, 2008 (effect size .41 on civic commitment); Ballard et al., 2016

Community colleges sit at the center of all of this. They enroll the very students the K–12 opportunity gap already passed over, with the least campus infrastructure to compensate. The students who most need what the government course was mandated to provide are the least likely to find it anywhere else. One required course. One guaranteed shot. That’s the pressure point this dissertation pushes on.

Exhibit D · The Wager

What if the course stopped describing citizenship and started requiring it?

First, name the culprit. In the traditional course, as much as 40% of the grade rides on high-stakes, multiple-choice exams. That format does two things badly at once: it measures memorization instead of understanding, and it teaches students that government is something to observe from a distance rather than something they do. The exam is not a neutral measuring stick. It is a lesson, and the lesson is: stay in your seat.

The intervention is called the Civic Action Project, and the idea fits in one sentence: replace the exams with the real thing.

In a CAP course, that same 40% doesn’t come from tests. It comes from a menu of 39 real civic activities across three effort tiers. Students choose their own path to 100 points. Attend the city council meeting. Write the representative. Work the food bank. Interview an election judge. Register to vote, then help someone else do it. The course stops assuming students already know how to participate and starts teaching participation the way we teach everything else that matters: by having them do it.

None of this is guesswork. The design sits on established science:

Self-Determination Theory · choice fuels motivation Kolb · experience → reflection → learning Action civics · doing builds civic identity Choice research · d ≈ .30 on intrinsic motivation 62-study meta-analysis · strongest effect: academic achievement

And to the inevitable question, “sounds engaging, but is it rigorous?”: a meta-analysis of 62 service-learning studies found significant positive effects across five outcome areas, and the strongest effect of all was on academic achievement. Experiential civic learning doesn’t trade rigor for relevance. The evidence says it delivers both.

Celio, Durlak, & Dymnicki, 2011

Choice isn’t a gimmick here; it’s the mechanism. Meta-analytic research shows student choice reliably raises intrinsic motivation, and the community-college study closest to this one found that taking a government course, inside an institution that treats civic engagement as intentional, predicts higher civic agency, capacity, behavior, and knowledge. CAP is that intentionality, built directly into the state’s own required course. No new money. No new course. No legislation. Just a redesigned 40% of a class Texas already requires.

Patall et al., 2008; Ryan & Deci, 2000; Kisker et al., 2016
Exhibit E · Early Returns

This isn’t hypothetical. It’s already running.

CAP has already run in real TCC classrooms. In Fall 2025, 75 students finished a full semester of it and told us what happened.

93% said CAP helped them understand government better than traditional study methods. CAP Reflection Survey, Fall 2025 · n=75
0% said it was worse. Not one student out of seventy-five. CAP Reflection Survey, Fall 2025
85% rated the overall CAP experience Good or Excellent, and the same share found the 100-point requirement manageable. CAP Reflection Survey, Fall 2025

Survey numbers are one kind of evidence. Here is the other kind.

Read that first one again. A student telling you that this is the first time school has ever stuck is the entire problem of practice and its answer, in two sentences. To be clear about what this evidence is and isn’t: these are self-reported perceptions from one pilot semester, not measured outcomes. They prove the model is feasible, welcomed, and workable at this institution with these students. What they cannot yet prove is that civic capacity actually moved. That is precisely the job of the study.

Exhibit F · The Proof

Then we measure it. Honestly.

Here is the strange truth about this whole field: almost all civic-outcomes research in higher education has happened at four-year and liberal-arts campuses. Very few studies have measured civic outcomes at community colleges at all, and the big national datasets tend to miss this population entirely: older students, recent immigrants, students whose civic lives don’t fit the standard checkboxes.

Kisker et al., 2016; Alcantar, 2014; Lopez & Brown, 2006

So the study builds the yardstick it needs: the Government Engagement Assessment (GEA), administered before and after the course in a quasi-experimental design. And it measures civic engagement the way the research says it must be measured for this population: not just voting, but social civic life too, the mentoring, translating, and community work that voting-centered surveys turn invisible. Measured too narrowly, engaged students look apathetic. This instrument is built not to make that mistake.

If CAP moves the needle…

Texas gets an evidence-backed, zero-cost model for activating a mandate it has carried since 1929, ready to travel through the very course every public college already teaches.

If it doesn’t…

The field gets something it has almost none of: rigorous civic-outcomes data from a community college HSI, plus an instrument others can use. Either answer is a contribution. That’s the mark of a question worth asking.

Exhibit G · The Reach

Built to travel.

A fix that lives in one instructor’s classroom isn’t a fix; it’s a fluke. So this project treats spread as part of the design, not an afterthought. The full CAP system, all 39 activities, rubrics, planning tools, and tracking, is published as an open educational resource: free, forkable, and adaptable by any instructor at any institution. A Texas Government companion, Texas In Action, extends the model to GOVT 2306. The diffusion strand of the dissertation draws on decades of research about how innovations actually move between faculty, so the model spreads by design rather than by luck.

Rogers, 2003; Foote et al., 2014; Porter & Graham, 2016

The scale case writes itself. Every degree-seeking student at every public college in Texas takes these courses. The delivery infrastructure has existed since 1929. The only question this dissertation leaves open is whether we choose to use it.

Closing · The Case, Three Ways

This is a contribution three times over.

To a neighbor

Every college kid in Texas has to take two government classes. The state says those classes should turn them into capable citizens. Nobody has ever checked if they do. I redesigned mine so students practice citizenship instead of memorizing it, and now I’m measuring whether it works.

To a committee

A quasi-experimental pre/post study of a choice-based, action-civics redesign of the state-mandated government courses at a commuter Hispanic-Serving Institution, measured with a purpose-built instrument. The intervention is already piloted, with a semester of implementation data demonstrating feasibility and strong student reception. It targets a documented evidentiary vacuum in community-college civic outcomes, operationalizes servingness as measurable practice, and carries a diffusion strand so the findings ship as an open resource rather than sit on a shelf. Grounded theory, demonstrated feasibility, honest measurement, built-in reach.

To the field

Three deliverables, each usable with or without the others: evidence where there is nearly none, an instrument tuned to a population national datasets miss, and a zero-cost, statute-compatible course model any government instructor can adopt Monday morning. That’s a problem of practice answered at the point of practice.

Respectfully submitted Fort Worth, Texas
