The four steps, one at a time, on a clock. Then you decide.

The four steps, written out

1 · Name it

Something hit you wrong. That first jolt is automatic. Everyone gets it; it isn't a failure. Anger is what happens if you agree with it. You haven't yet.

2 · Sort it

What part of this is actually mine? Not their motives. Not what they think of me. Just my next sentence, my tone, and whether I handle it now or in an hour.

3 · Ask the question

Was I actually harmed, or is this my idea of how things ought to go? Is my character damaged? If not, nothing that counts has been taken. And if the flare is bigger than the thing deserves, that's pointing at me, not them.

4 · Act, don't react

If it's a real wrong and it's yours to handle, handle it. In writing, in an hour, with the heat out of it. You're supposed to care about this. Anger just isn't what gets it fixed. It's what makes you miss.

The whole idea in one line

Your character is the only thing that really counts. Your health, your title, your reputation: all worth having, none of them worth trading your character for.

The Stoics said good character has four parts: wisdom, justice, courage, and self-control. Look at the second one. Caring about injustice isn't a distraction from this. It's a quarter of the whole thing.

The flare isn't the anger

Seneca drew a line between the first jolt, the hot rush when something hits you wrong, and actual anger. The jolt is automatic. It happens to everyone, including the people who are good at this.

Anger comes a half-second later, when you agree with the jolt: this is an outrage and I have to answer it right now. That agreement is the part you control. That's the only part you're training.

And the reason to skip it isn't that anger is unseemly. It's that anger is bad at the job. It gets away from you, it blows things out of proportion, and it overshoots. The angry move and the right move almost never turn out to be the same move.

Walter Sobchak is the proof. Never wrong about the rules, always wrong about the response. Being right and acting right are two different skills, and the second one is the one you're training.

The trap if you're in charge of things

"Not in my control" can quietly turn into an excuse to let a real wrong stand. That's not what any of them meant. Marcus ran an empire and still spent his nights on this.

Here's the cleaner line. What other people do with it isn't yours: whether the dean agrees, whether your colleague changes, whether your kid listens. What you decide and how you act is yours, and nobody can take that part.

So the question at the end of the day isn't "did I stay calm." It's: was that the right thing to do, and did my temper get in the way of doing it well?

What Jung adds

Jung wasn't a Stoic, and this app doesn't pretend he was. But he answers a question the Stoics leave open: once you've declined the flare, where do you look?

His answer: at yourself. What irritates you most in someone else is usually something you haven't fully admitted in yourself. The colleague who won't follow through, the student who doesn't care, the kid who won't listen. When the heat is out of proportion to the offense, that's the tell.

So the flare is information. Not about them. The Stoics give you the half-second; Jung tells you what to do with it. It also turns the evening review from a list of other people's faults into something you can act on.

Where Taoism meets Stoicism

They never met. Laozi and Zhuangzi are a century before Zeno, on the other side of the world. But they arrived at the same place from different roads: live in accord with the way things are, stop demanding the world go your way, and the sage is the one who isn't knocked over.

Where they split is on effort. Wu wei means not forcing. Water wears down stone; the carver follows the grain. Stoicism is more muscular. Justice is a duty, Marcus runs an empire, the sage shows up to the fight. A Taoist might think "address the wrong, with the heat out of it" is still too much pushing.

The useful split, for this app: Taoism is better on how to act. Don't contend, yield and persist, deal with the thing while it's small. Stoicism is better on whether to act. Yes, when it's yours. And Zhuangzi's empty boat is the best single image of anger in any tradition: you only shout at the boat if you believe someone's in it.

Pop culture got there too. The Dude abides. He gets mistaken for a nihilist, but the nihilists believe in nothing and the Dude minds about the rug. He just doesn't let the minding run him. That's wu wei with a bowling league, and it's the cleanest answer to the indifference trap there is: abiding isn't not caring.

Ring psychology

A glossary for anyone who learned about human nature on Saturday mornings. The terms are real; the business has used them for a century.

  • Heat. The crowd's anger, built on purpose so they'll pay to see the heel lose. Your flare is somebody's heat. The question is whether you're the crowd or the booker.
  • Mark. A fan who believes the work is a shoot. When you accept "this is an outrage and I must answer it now" without checking, you're a mark for your own anger.
  • Work and shoot. A work is the planned story; a shoot is real. The jolt is a shoot. The story it tells you about the other person is usually a work. The skill is telling them apart in real time.
  • Selling. Making the move look like it hurt. Good workers sell honestly and keep wrestling. No-selling is denial; over-selling is the judgment. Sell it. Stay in the match.
  • The hot tag. The fresh partner comes in after the crowd has waited for it. That's the whole payoff. When you're gassed in the corner, reach for the tag. Sixty seconds is the tag.
  • Putting someone over. Losing on purpose so the other person becomes a star. Teaching. Parenting. The best in the business did it every night and nobody thought less of them.
  • Going into business for yourself. Breaking the plan to make yourself look good. The match suffers. The leader who has to win every exchange is doing exactly this.
  • Working stiff. Hitting for real. It gets the reaction and hurts your partner. The best technicians got the same reaction working light. That's a leader.
  • The Dusty finish. The win that gets reversed on a technicality. Fans learned not to bank on the decision. Judge the match you worked, not the result.
Four habits, best one first
  • Don't act while hot. Wait. That's it. Everything else here is downstream of this one.
  • Call your shot in the morning. Name what's going to annoy you today. Getting ambushed is what makes you snap.
  • Look back at night. Right, miss, fix. Two minutes. Game film, not a punishment.
  • Zoom out. Will this matter in a year? Usually that answers it.
One rule for the evening

Marcus was hard on himself but never cruel about it. If the evening review becomes a list of everything you blew, you'll quit in a week. Start with one thing you did right. Then the miss. Then the fix. That order.
