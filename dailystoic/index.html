    Evening review — keep the order

    Right first, then the miss, then the fix. Reversing it is how people quit.

    Saved on this device only.

    in  ·  out

    Breathe with the circle. Five rounds.

    1 · Name it

    Something hit you wrong. That first jolt is automatic — everyone gets it, it's not a failure. Anger is what happens if you agree with it. You haven't yet.

    2 · Sort it

    What part of this is actually mine? Not their motives. Not what they think of me. Just my next sentence, my tone, and whether I handle it now or in an hour.

    3 · Ask Marcus's question

    Was I actually harmed, or is this my idea of how things ought to go? Is my character damaged? If not, nothing that counts has been taken from me.

    4 · Act, don't react

    If it's a real wrong and it's yours to handle, handle it — in writing, in an hour, with the heat out of it. You're supposed to care about this stuff. Anger just isn't what gets it fixed. It's what makes you miss.

    The last seven days

    What keeps showing up?

    The nightly review catches days. This catches patterns — the same person, the same hour, the same trigger, three weeks running.

    Sundays are a good time for this.

    The whole idea in one line

    Your character is the only thing that really counts. Your health, your title, your reputation — all worth having, none of them worth trading your character for.

    The Stoics said good character has four parts: wisdom, justice, courage, and self-control. Look at the second one. Caring about injustice isn't a distraction from this. It's a quarter of the whole thing.

    The flare isn't the anger

    Seneca drew a line between the first jolt — the hot rush when something hits you wrong — and actual anger. The jolt is automatic. It happens to everyone, including the people who are good at this.

    Anger comes a half-second later, when you agree with the jolt: this is an outrage and I have to answer it right now. That agreement is the part you control. That's the only part you're training.

    And the reason to skip it isn't that anger is unseemly. It's that anger is bad at the job. It gets away from you, it blows things out of proportion, and it overshoots. The angry move and the right move almost never turn out to be the same move.

    The trap if you're in charge of things

    "Not in my control" can quietly turn into an excuse to let a real wrong stand. That's not what any of them meant. Marcus ran an empire and still spent his nights on this.

    Here's the cleaner line. What other people do with it isn't yours: whether the dean agrees, whether your colleague changes, whether your kid listens. What you decide and how you act is yours, and nobody can take that part.

    So the question at the end of the day isn't "did I stay calm." It's: was that the right thing to do, and did my temper get in the way of doing it well?

    Four habits, best one first

    • Don't act while hot. Wait. That's it. Everything else here is downstream of this one.
    • Call your shot in the morning. Name what's going to annoy you today. Getting ambushed is what makes you snap.
    • Look back at night. Three questions, two minutes. Game film, not a punishment.
    • Zoom out. Will this matter in a year? Usually that answers it.

    One rule for the evening

    Marcus was hard on himself but never cruel about it. If the evening review becomes a list of everything you blew, you'll quit in a week. Start with one thing you did right. Then the miss. Then the fix. That order.
